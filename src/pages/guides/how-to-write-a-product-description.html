How to Write a Clear Product Description Without Inventing Claims

A good product description does the job of a helpful shop assistant: it answers questions before people need to ask them. This guide shows you how to write one from facts you already have.

Published October 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Step 1: Collect your facts first

Before writing anything, list what you know for certain about the product. Check the packaging, supplier specifications, and your own measurements.

  • What it is: the plain category (“ceramic mug”, “canvas tote bag”).
  • Size and weight, with units.
  • Materials, exactly as your supplier documents them.
  • What is included in the box or order.
  • How to use and care for it: washing, charging, storage.
  • Variants: colors, sizes, scents.

If you are not sure about something, find out or leave it out. A missing detail is better than a wrong one.

Step 2: Answer the questions shoppers ask

Look at the questions customers send you, the reviews on similar products, and your returns. Common questions include “Will it fit?”, “What is it made of?”, “How do I clean it?”, and “Is it a good gift?”. Each answer you add to the description is one less reason to hesitate, and one less return.

Step 3: Use a scannable structure

  1. A clear title: product name plus what it is, and one key detail if there is room. “Harvest Glow Soy Candle – 8 oz Glass Jar” is clearer than “Harvest Glow”.
  2. A one- or two-sentence summary that says what it is and who it is for.
  3. Feature bullets: short, factual, and easy to scan.
  4. A longer description for people who want more detail: how to use it, care instructions, and what makes it different.
  5. A call to action, such as “Add to cart” or “Choose your scent”.
Example summary

Harvest Glow is a scented soy candle for anyone who loves a cozy home. It comes in a reusable glass jar and burns for about 40 hours.

Step 4: Use the words people search for

Think about what a shopper would type into a search bar: “soy candle”, “autumn candle gift”, “vanilla candle”. Use those words naturally in the title and summary where they are accurate. Do not repeat the same phrase over and over. It reads badly and does not help people.

Step 5: Avoid claims you cannot prove

This is where many small sellers get into trouble. Advertising rules in many countries require that objective claims are truthful and can be backed up. The details vary by country and product type, but these kinds of claims deserve extra care:

Type of claimExampleWhat to do
Health or medical“Relieves anxiety”, “boosts immunity”Avoid unless you have the evidence and approvals your market requires.
Certifications“Organic”, “FDA approved”, “CE certified”Only use with valid certification documents for that product.
Environmental“Eco-friendly”, “biodegradable”Be specific (“recyclable glass jar”) and able to show proof.
Superlatives“The best”, “#1”, “longest-lasting”Replace with facts (“burns for about 40 hours”).
Guarantees“100% satisfaction guaranteed”Only if you have a real written policy that matches.

When in doubt, describe the fact instead of the promise. “Made with soy wax” is a fact. “Healthier than other candles” is a claim that needs evidence.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Check the advertising rules for your country and the policies of the marketplace you sell on.

Final checklist

  • Is every feature something you could prove if a customer asked?
  • Can a shopper tell the size, material, and what is included in under ten seconds?
  • Does the title say what the product is?
  • Have you answered the most common customer question?
  • Did you read it on a phone screen?
