How to Write Strong Resume Bullet Points

Most people reading a resume skim it first. Clear bullet points help them see what you did and why it mattered. This guide gives you a repeatable method, with examples you can adapt.

Published October 20, 2026 · 7 min read

The three-part formula

A strong bullet usually answers three questions in one line:

  1. What did you do? Start with a specific action verb in the past tense (or present tense for your current job).
  2. What did you do it to, or for? Name the task, project, customer group, or system.
  3. What changed because of it? Add a result, scale, or context, but only if it is true and you could explain it in an interview.

Action + what + result or scale
“Reorganized the stockroom layout, cutting the time to find items during busy shifts.”

Not every bullet needs a number. Some duties are simply part of the job, and that is fine. Aim for a mix: a few bullets with clear outcomes, and a few that show the range of your responsibilities.

Start with a specific verb

Bullets that start with “Responsible for”, “Helped with”, or “Worked on” tell the reader that you were nearby, not what you did. Replace them with the verb that describes your actual contribution.

Instead ofTry (if it is accurate)
Responsible for the team scheduleCreated / Managed / Updated the weekly team schedule
Helped with eventsOrganized / Coordinated / Set up company events
Worked on the websiteWrote / Redesigned / Tested pages on the company website
Dealt with complaintsResolved / Responded to / Escalated customer complaints

Pick the verb that is true. “Led” and “managed” suggest you were in charge. If you supported someone else's project, “supported” or “contributed to” is the honest choice, and an interviewer will respect that.

Before and after examples

Before

Responsible for social media.

After

Planned and published three Instagram posts a week for a local bakery, using Canva for graphics.

Before

Helped customers.

After

Answered customer questions in person and by phone, and processed returns and exchanges at the service desk.

Before

Did data entry.

After

Entered and checked supplier invoices in the accounting system, and flagged mismatches to the finance manager.

Notice that none of the “after” versions adds anything that did not happen. They are better because they are specific: what platform, which customers, what system, and what you did when something was wrong.

Adding numbers honestly

Numbers help readers understand scale. Good sources of honest numbers include:

  • Volume: how many customers, tickets, orders, or students you handled in a typical day or week.
  • Size: the size of the team, budget, or project, or the number of locations.
  • Frequency: daily, weekly, or monthly tasks.
  • Before and after: a process that took two days and then took one. Use this only if you saw the change yourself.

If you are estimating, say so with words like “about” or “around”, or give a range (“20–30 calls a day”). Never invent a percentage. If someone asks how you measured it, you should have an answer.

Common mistakes

  • Copying the job description. Your old job ad lists duties. Your resume should show what you actually did.
  • Very long bullets. If a bullet runs past two lines, split it or cut the least important detail.
  • Mixing tenses. Keep one tense per job: present for your current role, past for previous ones.
  • Using “I” and “my”. Resume bullets usually leave out the pronoun: “Managed…”, not “I managed…”.
  • The same verb every time. Five bullets starting with “Managed” blur together. Vary them when a different verb is still accurate.
  • Overstating your role. It is tempting, but it can backfire in an interview or a reference check.

Tailor bullets to each application

Read the job posting and note the tasks and skills it mentions most often. Then move your most relevant bullets to the top of each role, and use the employer's wording where it truthfully describes your experience. You do not need to rewrite your whole resume each time. Reordering and adjusting a few bullets is often enough.
